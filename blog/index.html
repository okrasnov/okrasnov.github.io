Today is November 13, 2026. Did Heinz von Foerster’s Infamous Doomsday Math Come True?

Look around.

If a group of pioneering physicists led by Heinz von Foerster were right when they published a jaw-dropping paper in the journal Science back in 1960, today is the day.

Titled "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026," their paper calculated that on this exact calendar date, the global human population would reach infinity. Humanity wouldn’t go out in a fiery nuclear apocalypse or a cataclysmic asteroid impact; instead, von Foerster cheekily predicted we would simply be “squeezed to death” as a hyper-accelerating population hit a mathematical brick wall of infinite density.

Well, November 13, 2026 is finally here. We aren’t being squeezed out of existence, and grocery stores are still standing. So, how did a team of brilliant scientists end up with an equation that predicted a population singularity for today? More importantly, where did the math go wrong?

Let’s take a deep dive into the math, the satire, and the real-world demographics that saved us from hyperbolic collapse.

1. The Core Premise: Interaction Density

Standard population growth models assume growth scales linearly with population size—the more people there are, the more babies are born. But von Foerster argued this baseline missed a crucial human trait: cooperation and technological innovation.

Human progress accelerates because people interact, share ideas, and invent better ways to survive. As the population grows, the network of human interactions expands even faster. More interaction leads to better technology, higher living standards, and accelerated growth rates.

3. Why the Math Failed: Reality Hits Back

Von Foerster’s paper was largely a brilliant, satirical critique of unchecked demographic extrapolation. It demonstrated that assuming endless positive feedback in physical systems leads to absurdity.

Two major real-world mechanisms broke the hyperbolic model almost immediately after it was published.

A. Carrying Capacity () and the Logistic Shift

In ecological systems, biological populations do not grow unchecked; they are bounded by their environment’s carrying capacity (). Population biology models this using the logistic equation (Verhulst model):

  • Hyperbolic Model: Driven by positive feedback ( ). Higher population accelerates growth infinitely.
  • Logistic Model: Driven by negative feedback. As approaches , the multiplier approaches zero, bringing growth to a halt and establishing a horizontal asymptote at .

While agricultural breakthroughs like the Green Revolution continuously raised Earth’s carrying capacity (), physical resources still act as a ceiling rather than an infinite ramp.

B. The Demographic Transition Model (DTM)

The primary reason von Foerster’s curve shattered was not a sudden lack of food, but a fundamental shift in human behavior: the Demographic Transition.

Von Foerster published his paper at the tail end of Stage 2 of global industrialization - a phase characterized by plummeting mortality rates alongside high birth rates. He mistook a temporary historical acceleration for a permanent law of nature.

Irony struck almost immediately:

  • The 1962–1963 Peak: Just two years after the paper was published, the global annual population growth rate peaked at ~2.2% per year.
  • Deceleration: Since the late 1960s, the global growth rate has continuously fallen. Today, it stands below 1.0%.

As societies industrialize, urbanize, and expand access to female education and economic opportunities, total fertility rates (TFR) fall dramatically. Higher density and economic development cause families to voluntarily choose smaller family sizes—the exact opposite of von Foerster’s assumption that density accelerates birth rates.

4. Von Foerster vs. Modern UN Projections

Today, demographic modeling has evolved from naive curve-fitting into complex cohort-component analysis tracking regional fertility, mortality, and migration rates.

Comparing von Foerster’s model to the United Nations World Population Prospects (2024 Revision) highlights the contrast between unbounded extrapolation and dynamic modeling:

Conclusion: The Ultimate Paradox

Heinz von Foerster’s 1960 paper remains one of the most brilliant pieces of mathematical satire ever published in serious scientific literature. By taking historical population trends to their logical mathematical extreme, he delivered a timeless warning: no dynamic trend can continue unchecked forever.

We survived November 13, 2026 without getting squeezed to death. Instead, humanity reacted to its own growth through voluntary behavioral changes, economic shifts, and declining fertility rates - turning a hyperbolic path to doomsday into a stable, manageable plateau.

80 years ago we received our own signals from outer space for the first time

On January 10, 1946, a team of military and civilian personnel of the Project Diana at Camp Evans, Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, aimed a modified SCR-271 bedspring radar system at the rising Moon and transmitted high-power radio pulses in its direction. About 2.5 seconds later, the range-amplitude oscilloscope’s indicator for the first time in human history registered a faint radio echo that travelled to the Moon through the Earth’s atmosphere, outer space, reflected from the Moon’s surface, and returned to Earth.

The radar was operating at the frequency 111.5 MHz, transmitted 15 kW pulses with 250 microsecond duration at a 1/3 Hz pulse repetition rate (one pulse per 3 seconds). It uses an enormous (40x40-foot, 12.2x12.2 meters) double bedspring antenna consisting of an 8x8 array of 64 half-wavelength dipoles with reflectors. Such an array has an antenna pattern with 15 degrees main beam and provides a still not too high gain of about 23 dB (200 times). In available specification (unfortunately, engineering specifications were destroyed by the Army in 1971, so the exact design details are unknown), the maximum Doppler shift of echo was mentioned as 327 Hz with a comment that it was set in the radar for a target at maximum range 980 MPH = 1577,16 km/h = 438,1 m/s (it has the exact Doppler frequency shift 326 Hz at 111.5 MHz frequency)…

More details about the project Diana can be found in the following sources:

  • A personal website that is based on materials of the project’s veteran E. King Stodola
  • Wikipedia
  • an article “Radar Reaches the Moon” by Tom Gootee in the magazine Radio News, vol.35(4), April 1946 (available at WorldRadioHistory.Com)
